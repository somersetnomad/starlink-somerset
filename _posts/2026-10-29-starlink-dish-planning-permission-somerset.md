<div class="answer" markdown="1">
**Short answer:** for most houses in Somerset, no. Fitting a Starlink dish is "permitted development", so you can go ahead without applying. You will need permission or consent if **your home is listed**, if you're in a **conservation area, Exmoor National Park or one of Somerset's National Landscapes** and want the dish on a wall or roof facing the road, or if the dish would **stick up above your roof or chimney** beyond the limits below.
</div>

The rules come from Class H of the [General Permitted Development Order 2015](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/596/schedule/2/part/1/crossheading/class-h-microwave-antenna-on-a-dwellinghouse), and they apply to houses in England.[^1]

## Why Starlink counts as a large antenna

This is the detail most guides miss. The rules treat antennas up to 60 cm long more generously than bigger ones, and they measure length in any straight line, not counting the mount or brackets.

Starlink's own [specification sheet](https://www.starlink.com/public-files/specification_sheet_standard_es-ES.pdf) gives the standard dish as 594 × 383 mm. That's just under 60 cm along its long edge, but measured corner to corner it's about **71 cm**.[^2] So treat it as the larger kind of antenna. That matters in three ways:

1. **No chimney mount without permission.** Antennas on a chimney must be no longer than 60 cm, and must not stick up above the chimney.
2. **Only one per house.** You can have up to two antennas, but only one of them may be over 60 cm. If you already have a big old dish on the house, it would need to come down first, or you'd need permission.
3. **It's well inside the other limits.** The maximum length for any antenna is 1 metre and the maximum volume 35 litres.[^3]

This is a careful reading of the legislation's wording, not a legal ruling.[^4]

## How high the dish can go

Height is where pole and mast mounts catch people out. Under Class H:

- **On a roof without a chimney**, the highest part of the dish can't be higher than the highest part of the roof.
- **On a roof with a chimney**, it can't be higher than the chimney, or 60 cm above the ridge tiles, whichever is lower.

Starlink needs a clear view of the sky, so it's tempting to raise it on a tall pole above the ridge. If that takes it past these limits, you need planning permission. Often a gable wall or garden mount sees enough sky.[^5]

## Conservation areas, Exmoor and the hills

Planning law gives extra protection to [designated areas](https://planningportal.co.uk/permission/common-projects/satellite-tv-and-radio-antenna): conservation areas, National Parks and National Landscapes.[^6] In Somerset that includes the county's conservation areas, Exmoor National Park, and the Mendip Hills, Quantock Hills and Blackdown Hills.

On designated land you can still have Starlink without permission. The extra rule in Class H is that the dish **can't go on a chimney, wall or roof slope that faces onto, and is visible from, a road**. A rear roof slope, a rear wall or the back garden is usually the answer.

### How to check your address

Somerset Council keeps [conservation area maps and appraisals](https://somerset.gov.uk/conservation-areas-maps-and-appraisals), sorted by the old district council areas.[^7] Pick your district or enter your postcode.

## Listed buildings

If your home is listed, at any grade, assume you need **listed building consent** before fixing a dish to it. The [Planning Portal](https://planningportal.co.uk/permission/common-projects/satellite-tv-and-radio-antenna) says consent is needed for any antenna that affects the character or appearance of a listed building or its setting.[^8] Doing listed building work without consent can be a criminal offence, so this is the one rule not to guess at.

You can check whether your home is listed on [Historic England's National Heritage List](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/), or ask the council if you're unsure.

A free-standing dish in the garden may be easier to get approved than one on the building, but it can still affect the building's setting. Talk to the council's conservation team before you order.

## Two conditions that always apply

Even when you don't need permission, Class H comes with two conditions:

- **Site it to minimise its effect** on how the building looks, as far as practicable.
- **Take it down** when you no longer use it, as soon as reasonably practicable.

## Flats, rented homes and leaseholds

Class H covers houses. If you live in a flat, the [Planning Portal's antenna guide](https://planningportal.co.uk/permission/common-projects/satellite-tv-and-radio-antenna) sets out the separate rules. Whatever kind of home you're in, if you rent or own on a lease, you'll also need permission from your landlord or freeholder before fixing anything to the building.

## If you get it wrong

If a dish needed permission and didn't have it, the council can ask for a retrospective application. If that's refused, it can require you to move or remove the dish at your own expense.[^9] For most houses the fix is simple: move the dish to a rear slope, wall or the garden.

## Questions people ask

### Can I put Starlink on my chimney?

Not under permitted development in most cases. Chimney antennas are limited to 60 cm, and the standard dish is about 71 cm corner to corner.

### Do I need permission on a listed building?

Almost certainly. Assume you need listed building consent and speak to Somerset Council's conservation team before installing anything.

### Can I have Starlink and a Sky dish?

Usually yes. You can have two antennas, but only one may be over 60 cm. Starlink takes that slot, so a second large dish would need permission.

### Does a conservation area rule Starlink out?

No. The dish just can't go on a chimney, wall or roof slope facing and visible from a road. Rear slopes, rear walls and gardens are usually fine.

### Will the council make me take it down?

Only if it needed permission and the council refuses a retrospective application. Then they can require you to move or remove it.
